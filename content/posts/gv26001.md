title_zh: 外派苏丹突遭车祸的日子
excerpt_zh: |
  人生第一份工作，第一次出国，第一个项目。新鲜劲还没过，翻车了
body_zh: >-
  直到今天，我吃饭时左手还不怎么喜欢扶碗。不是不想，是一根锁骨不让。


  ![](/public/images/uploads/screenshot_20261021_150307_com.huawei.hmos.photos.jpg)


  2011年，我大学毕业，进了一家国企做翻译。外派手续三下五除二办完，打过疫苗，拿到红黄本，就跟同事飞到了苏丹首都喀土穆。


  人生第一份工作，第一次出国，第一个项目。


  **新鲜劲还没过，翻车了。**


  那次，我作为翻译陪中方同事勘察项目现场。


  苏丹当时基础设施薄弱，出了喀土穆，手机就没信号了。除了国家级主干道是柏油路，剩下几乎全是土路。车跑了一天，终于晃回能接收信号的地方。司机大概是累了，在临近村庄一个急弯，方向一打，刹车一踩，车瞬间失控横滚。


  那是辆丰田Hilux皮卡，双排座。副驾坐着喀土穆大学一位女教授。后排有我、中方同事和一名苏丹工作人员。司机是当地人，圆圆的脑袋，身材敦实。


  事后回忆，只能想起，我在车里翻了个，接着就是眼前一黑。再回过神，也不知道过了多久，车四轮朝天，音乐还在放。


  第一个担心就是怕车爆炸，想快点出去，但浑身没劲，连撑一下都做不到。


  好在玻璃碎了，村民围拢过来，有人把我从车窗中拽了出来。


  我赶忙摸了脸和胳膊，又摸了摸腿，**还好都在，长出一口气。**


  悬着的心刚放下，左肩的疼就上来了。想抬左臂，没成功，反而疼得呲牙咧嘴。


  我示意同事看看。他皱着眉说，肩膀肿了。我扭着脖子看去，像是肩上塞了个拳头。


  公司来车把我们送去了当地新开的公立医院，拍了片子。其他人没什么大碍，我锁骨粉碎性骨折，得手术。


  斜靠在轮椅上，我疼得不敢动弹。赶来医院汇合的领导说，公司紧急研究了两套方案，想听听我的意见：一是飞回国内做手术；二是留在苏丹，找当地条件最好的中资医院，用国内的医生主刀。


  我同意了第二种。**当时脑子里只有一个念头：越早做手术越好。**


  全麻，手术从第二天早晨做到下午。醒来后，左胳膊连同左肩已被固定好。主刀大夫对我说，左侧锁骨中外三分之一处粉碎性骨折，很幸运，没有伤到肺。手术做得很细致，上下各夹了两块钢板、打了钉子。


  那天，正好我生日。


  医护人员和同事们帮我张罗了蛋糕，煮了碗面，权当是长寿面，大家还围着病床给我唱了生日歌。**那个场面现在想起来有点滑稽，当时也笑，笑完还是觉得低落。**


  20来岁的我，一个人在苏丹，左肩里多了两块钢板。


  住院的日子，公司安排了中国同事陪护，翻不了身的那几天喂饭、照料得很悉心。直到现在，我一直很感激他。


  怕家人担心，直到手术做完我也没说这个事。后来QQ语音聊天，家人感觉我情绪不对，追问后才了解到实情。我妈在电话那头含泪催促，立刻回国！


  我打着绷带飞回了首都机场，开始了一段在家养伤的日子。


  那段时间，我把在苏丹所有的诊断证明、片子、治疗材料整理好，去人社部门递交了材料。


  **我得给自己一个交代。**


  被认定为工伤九级后，一次性拿了九万多。那是我上班第一年，拿到的最大一笔现金。自那以后，我把它当成人生的"第一桶金"。


  一根锁骨，换来的。


  赔偿结束后，我平静地离了职。公司挽留过，我没回头。


  那根锁骨，让我把一些事想清楚了。


  后来，取钢板又挨了一刀。骨头愈合了，但有些东西永远回不去了。吃饭时，我的左肩还会下意识地往回缩。**有些东西，身体记得比脑子清楚。**


  你的身体，替你记住了什么？
title_en: The day I suddenly had a car accident in Sudan
excerpt_en: First job, first time going abroad, first project. The initial
  excitement hasn't faded yet, but it's crashed.
body_en: >-
  Until today, my left hand still doesn't really like holding the bowl when I
  eat. It's not that I don't want to, but a single clavicle is preventing me.


  ![](/public/images/uploads/screenshot_20261021_150307_com.huawei.hmos.photos.jpg)


  In 2011, I graduated from university and joined a state-owned enterprise as a translator. The overseas assignment procedures were quickly handled, I got vaccinated and obtained the yellow fever certificate, then flew to Khartoum, the capital of Sudan, with my colleagues.


  First job in life, first time going abroad, first project.


  The freshness hasn't worn off yet, but it's already crashed.


  On that occasion, I served as a translator and accompanied my Chinese colleagues to inspect the project site.


  At the time, Sudan's infrastructure was underdeveloped, with no mobile signal available outside Khartoum. Except for the national trunk roads, which were asphalt, the rest were almost entirely dirt paths. The car drove for a day before finally bouncing back to an area with signal reception. The driver, likely exhausted, made an abrupt turn at a sharp bend near a village, suddenly applied the brakes, and the vehicle instantly lost control, rolling over sideways.


  It was a Toyota Hilux pickup truck with double-row seating. The passenger seat was occupied by a female professor from the University of Khartoum. In the back row were I, my Chinese colleague, and a Sudanese staff member. The driver was a local man with a round face and a sturdy build.


  Looking back on the incident, I can only recall that the car rolled over, and then everything went black. When I came to, I didn't know how much time had passed, but the car was upside down with all four wheels in the air, and the music was still playing.


  The first concern is fearing the car might explode, wanting to get out quickly, but feeling completely drained, unable to even stand up for a moment.


  Fortunately, the glass broke, and the villagers gathered around, with someone pulling me out of the car window.


  I hurriedly ran my hands over my face and arms, then over my legs again, and let out a breath of relief.


  The heart that had been full of anxiety just eased, but the pain in the left shoulder surged back. Trying to lift the left arm failed, and instead, the pain caused him to wince in agony.


  I gestured for my colleague to take a look. He frowned and said, "My shoulder is swollen." I twisted my neck to look, as if a fist had been stuffed into his shoulder.


  The company's vehicle took us to the newly opened public hospital in the local area, where we had X-rays taken. Others didn't have serious injuries, but I suffered a comminuted fracture of the clavicle and require surgery.


  Propped up against the wheelchair, I was in such pain I couldn't move. The leaders who arrived at the hospital to join me said the company had urgently studied two options and wanted to hear my opinion: first, to fly back home for surgery; second, to stay in Sudan and find the best Chinese-funded hospital there, with domestic doctors performing the operation.


  I agreed with the second option. **At that moment, the only thought in my mind was: The sooner the surgery, the better.**


  The surgery was performed under general anesthesia and lasted from morning to afternoon on the second day. Upon waking up, my left arm and shoulder were already immobilized. The lead surgeon told me that there was a comminuted fracture in the middle third of my left clavicle, which was fortunate as the lung was not injured. The surgery was conducted with great care, involving the insertion of two plates on both the upper and lower parts, along with screws.


  That day, it just happened to be my birthday.


  The medical staff and colleagues helped prepare a cake and cooked a bowl of noodles as a longevity noodle, and they also sang the birthday song around my hospital bed. Now that I think back, the scene was somewhat comical, but I also laughed at the time. However, even after laughing, I still felt down.


  At the age of around 20, I was alone in Sudan and had two steel plates inserted into my left shoulder.


  During my hospital stay, the company arranged for a Chinese colleague to accompany me. For the days when I couldn't turn over, he carefully fed me and provided meticulous care. I have always been very grateful to him to this day.


  I was afraid to worry my family, so I didn't mention this matter until after the surgery was completed. Later, during a QQ voice chat, my family sensed that my mood was off, and only after they pressed me for details did they learn the truth. My mother, on the other end of the phone, tearfully urged me, and I immediately returned to the country!


  I flew back to Capital International Airport with a bandage on, beginning a period of rest at home to recover.


  During that period, I organized all the diagnostic certificates, medical images, and treatment materials from Sudan and submitted them to the Human Resources and Social Security Bureau.


  I have to give myself an explanation.


  After being recognized as a level nine work-related injury, I received a one-time payment of over 90,000. That was the largest cash payment I had ever received in my first year of work. Since then, I've treated it as my life's first big chunk of money.


  Earned with a collarbone.


  After the compensation was settled, I calmly left my job. The company tried to retain me, but I didn't look back.


  That collarbone made me think through some things.


  Later, when the steel plate was removed, I was cut again. The bones healed, but some things will never return. Even during meals, my left shoulder still involuntarily pulls back. Some things, the body remembers more clearly than the mind.


  What has your body remembered for you?
