body_zh: >-
  ![](/public/images/uploads/screenshot_20261021_150253_com.huawei.hmos.photos.jpg)


  北京有个呼家楼，还有个安家楼，一字之差。一个收过我十来年的青春，一个拒了我的签证。


  我从呼家楼出发的那些年，包里背的是本公务护照。里面盖满了章。很多章上的字我不认识，只认得日期。


  那本护照存着我的旧身份。按规定，回国就要上交。它和我走了很多地方，但那些路，从来不是我的。我只是被派去走那些路的人。


  **走完了，路留下，人回来。**


  后来，我拿着因私护照，去了安家楼路55号。


  白本的，翻开什么记录都没有。


  面签前一天，我住到使馆附近的酒店。走廊里有人行色匆匆。半掩着的房门里漏出压着嗓子的说话声，语速很快，像是在跟谁交代着什么。


  和以往公派不同，这次是公司安排的临时出差，时间紧，还办了加急。


  第二天一早出门，深秋的北京，天很高，风很硬。


  使馆外排队的人比预想中多。风往领口里灌，拉链拉到顶还是漏。手机已提前存在外面，我的手无所事事插在兜里，跟着队伍往前挪。


  进入使馆，抬眼看到，等待的队伍排到了二楼挑空的护栏边。


  签证窗口前，有人转身时脚步轻快，有人低着头把材料塞回袋里，拉链拽了两次才拉上。


  有个窗口刚坐下的人，没聊几句就接过退回来的护照，一脸茫然。签证官招手叫下一个，我坐了过去。


  他打开护照，看着我，又往后翻了几页。然后问：“去美国做什么？”


  “去参加会议。”


  “最近几年出过国吗？”


  我的确出过。非洲、西亚、南亚、东南亚、拉美、东亚……


  不过，所有记录都在另一本护照里。它已经上交，和我的上段经历一起，锁进了档案袋。


  那一瞬间，我说了句：“没有。”


  为什么没讲实情？后来我也想过。怕太复杂？觉得说了也没用？还是某种说不清的倔……当时没想这些，就说了没有。


  他又问了问公司的情况。


  我一一作答。


  随后，他说：Sorry, I can't issue you visa this time.


  护照和签证官没看一眼的材料被推出来，还加了一封拒签信。说是信，其实是小小的一张纸，没有特别的拒签理由。


  一上午的排队，几分钟就出了结果。


  出来取回手机，我把信放进资料袋，没再看第二眼。


  签证官翻看的那本护照，空无一物。


  **他看不见的东西，我自己也拿不出来。**


  那本公务护照上交时，我最后翻过一遍。几十个章，一段都带不走。白本护照在我手里，轻得像什么都没发生过。


  回家路上，我望着北京天空的深秋蓝，通透，干净，风一吹更显凛冽。


  不知道你的护照里，是不是也藏着一段别人看不见的路。

  There is a Hujialou in Beijing, and there's also an Anjialou, differing by just one character. One has taken away my dozen years of youth, the other denied me a visa.


  The years I departed from Hujialou, the official passport I carried in my bag was covered with stamps. Many of the stamps had characters I didn't recognize, but I could make out the dates.


  That passport holds my old identity. As per regulations, it must be surrendered upon returning home. It has traveled with me to many places, but those paths were never mine. I was merely the person sent to walk those paths.


  The journey is done, the road remains, people return.


  Later, I went to No. 55 Anjiashou Road with a private passport.


  It's a blank one; when you open it, there's nothing in the records.


  The day before the visa interview, I stayed at a hotel near the embassy. People were hurrying down the corridor. Muffled voices came through the half-open doors, spoken in a low tone with rapid speech, as if explaining something to someone.


  Unlike previous official assignments, this time it's a temporary business trip arranged by the company. The time is tight, and they also applied for expedited processing.


  On the morning of the next day, Beijing in late autumn had a high sky and a harsh wind.


  The number of people queuing outside the embassy is greater than anticipated. The wind blows into my collar, and even when I zip my zipper all the way up, it still leaks through. My phone was already left outside, and my hands, idle, were stuck in my pockets, following the crowd forward.


  Upon entering the embassy, looking up, one could see that the waiting line had stretched to the edge of the railing in the second-floor open space.


  In front of the visa counter, some people turned around with light steps, while others lowered their heads and shoved the materials back into their bags, tugging the zipper twice before finally closing it.


  There was a person who had just sat down at the window, who received the returned passport without exchanging many words and looked bewildered. The visa officer waved to call the next person, and I took the seat.


  He opens his passport, looks at me, and flips back a few pages. Then he asks, "What are you going to the US for?"


  Go to the meeting.


  Have you been abroad in recent years?


  I have indeed been there. Africa, West Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Latin America, East Asia...


  However, all the records are in another passport. It has been submitted along with my previous experience and locked into an archive bag.


  At that moment, I said, "No."


  Why didn't you tell the truth? Later, I also thought about it. Afraid it was too complicated? Thought that saying it wouldn't help? Or some unexplainable stubbornness... At that time, I didn't think about these things, so I just said there was none.


  He again asked about the company's situation.


  I will answer one by one.


  Subsequently, he said: "Sorry, I can't issue you a visa this time."


  The materials that the passport and visa officers didn't even glance at were submitted, accompanied by a rejection letter. Though called a letter, it's actually just a small piece of paper with no specific reason given for the rejection.


  Waiting in line all morning, the results came out in just a few minutes.


  When I came out to retrieve my phone, I put the letter into the file folder and didn't look a second time.


  The passport the visa officer flipped through was empty.


  What he can't see, I can't get either.


  When I handed over the official passport, I went through it one last time. Dozens of stamps, not even a single page could be taken away. The blank passport in my hands was light as if nothing had happened.


  On my way home, I gazed at the deep autumn blue of Beijing's sky, crisp and clean. The wind made it even more biting.


  Don't you know that there's also a hidden path in your passport that others can't see?
