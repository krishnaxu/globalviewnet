  ![](/public/images/uploads/110678ff2cc43eead67c1ce59bb9ac3~tplv-tt-origin-web_gif.jpeg)


  今天是2026年十一小长假最后一天。


  下午窝在沙发上，翻看前两天在承德拍的照片。一张是避暑山庄里的水心榭，三座亭子架在湖上，水在底下慢慢淌，游人在廊下慢慢走。另一张是普陀宗乘之庙，红墙金顶，背后是山。


  看着看着，心里有点不是滋味。


  倒不是照片拍得多好，**而是拍完它们之后，时间就快进到今晚了。**


  假期额度清零，明天要上班。


  ![](/public/images/uploads/2d8216a2be68641fbb6846e04cf96d203~tplv-tt-origin-web_gif.jpeg)


  这次中秋连着国庆，我在家待了13天。工作地离家远，平时回来有限，这13天不夸张的说，是一整年里头最长的团圆之一。说是假期，其实也没彻底闲下来，前面几天在家一样处理了工作，消息没断过。


  放假前数着日子盼，放假后头两天睡到自然醒，觉得全世界都慢了。


  站在水心榭边上那天下午，风不大，水面泛着光，亭子的倒影晃啊晃。那会儿没想别的，就觉得能这么站着挺好的。上午去小布达拉宫爬台阶，累，但挺心甘情愿的，因为不用赶任何东西。


  现在想想，**假期的好，就是不用赶。**


  不用赶着回消息，不用赶着处理那些没完没了的事。哪怕只是跟着人群慢慢挪，那也是自己想停就停的节奏。


  今晚洗完澡，把明天的衣服摆出来，闹钟定好，就知道自己又该“回到正轨”了。


  其实从前天开始，身体就先不乐意了。头懵懵的，腰也酸疼，说不上病，就是整个人像被抽走了什么。大概是这13天松得太彻底，一下子要绷回去，身体先替自己抵抗了。


  此刻，大概多少还是有点不甘心。


  水心榭的水还在那儿淌着，小布达拉宫的红墙还在那儿晒着太阳，可我明天就得回到工位，回到那一堆邮件和消息里去。而且就算下次再去，也是另一种心情了——带着班味儿去的，和现在这种彻底松下来的，不一样。


  高铁上其实就已经开始了。工作群蹦出几条消息，看了，没回，但心里那根弦已经悄悄绷回去一点。回到住所更明显，快递堆在驿站，随手整理带来的衣服，把家人做的卤味放进冰箱，**所有东西都在提醒：你回来了。**


  明天闹钟一响，肯定懵。然后收拾，出门，打卡，开电脑。慢慢从“承德的湖边”回到“第几行表格”，大概需要一上午。


  也不是不能接受。就是每年每次到这一天，都还是会有点闷。


  ![](/public/images/uploads/3d0c315a983f849a3b349b2aad08480e4~tplv-tt-origin-web_gif.jpeg)


  照片在手机存好。哪天下午工作进行不下去了，点开看看，心里能好过一点。不是怀念，是提醒自己：你刚去过那种地方，你不是一直困在这儿的。


  假期到头就到头吧。


  只是希望下次累的时候，还能记得水心榭底下那汪水的样子。

  Today is the last day of the 2026 National Day holiday.


  This afternoon, I'm lounging on the couch, flipping through photos I took in Chengde the previous days. One photo shows the Water Heart Pavilion in the Summer Resort, with three pavilions built over the lake, water flowing slowly beneath, and pedestrians moving leisurely under the corridors. Another is of the Putuo Zongcheng Temple, with its red walls and golden roof, backed by mountains.


  Looking at these photos, I feel a bit melancholic.


  Not because the photos are particularly good, **but because after taking them, time quickly fast-forwards to tonight.**


  The holiday is over, and tomorrow I have to return to work.


  ![](/public/images/uploads/2d8216a2be68641fbb6846e04cf96d203~tplv-tt-origin-web_gif.jpeg)


  This Mid-Autumn Festival coincided with National Day, giving me 13 days at home. Since my workplace is far from home and I rarely come back, these 13 days are, without exaggeration, one of the longest family reunions of the year. Although it's called a holiday, I didn't completely rest; I still handled work matters at home, and messages never stopped coming.


  Before the holiday, I counted down the days, eagerly anticipating it. But after the first couple of days, I slept in and felt like the whole world had slowed down.


  That afternoon when I stood by the Water Heart Pavilion, the wind was gentle, the water shimmered, and the pavilions' reflections swayed. At that moment, I didn't think about anything else—just felt good about being there. The next morning, I climbed the steps to the Small Potala Palace, which was tiring, but I was willing to do it because there was no rush.


  Now thinking back, **the beauty of the holiday is that you don't have to rush.**


  No need to rush back to check messages or deal with endless tasks. Even just moving slowly with the crowd is a rhythm that allows me to stop whenever I want.


  After taking a bath tonight, I laid out tomorrow's clothes and set the alarm clock, knowing that I would soon be "back on track."


  Actually, my body started resisting from the day before yesterday. My head felt dizzy, my back ached, and I couldn't exactly say I was sick—it was more like something had been taken away from me entirely. Perhaps I had relaxed too thoroughly over these 13 days, and now, suddenly needing to tighten up again, my body resisted on its own.


  Right now, I'm still a bit unwilling to return.


  The water in Shui Xinxi is still flowing, and the red walls of the small Potala Palace are still basking in the sun, but tomorrow I'll have to go back to my desk, back into the pile of emails and messages. And even if I go back again next time, it'll be with a different mindset—carrying the "work flavor," which is different from this current state of complete relaxation.


  The high-speed rail journey itself had already begun. A few messages popped up in the work group, I glanced at them but didn't reply, yet the string in my heart had already started to tighten a little. Once back at the accommodation, it was even more obvious. The courier station was piled with packages, I casually sorted through the clothes I had brought, and put the braised delicacies my family made into the fridge. Everything around me was reminding me: you're back.


  When the alarm clock rings tomorrow, I'll definitely feel confused. Then I'll get up, get ready, go out, punch in, and open my computer. It will take me the whole morning to slowly transition from "by the lake in Chengde" back to "the nth row of a spreadsheet."


  It's not that I can't accept it. It's just that every year, every time this day comes, I still feel a bit down.


  ![](/public/images/uploads/3d0c315a983f849a3b349b2aad08480e4~tplv-tt-origin-web_gif.jpeg)


  The photo is saved on my phone. One afternoon, when work feels unbearable, I'll open it and it will ease my mind a bit. It's not nostalgia, but a reminder to myself: you've just been to that place, and you're not always trapped here.


  The holiday ends when it ends.
